Bibliography and Sources

This note collects the key books, articles, and online resources that underpin the Natural Dye and Eco Printing — Learning Journey. Sources are grouped by topic to match the steps they support. Where a DOI or stable URL exists, it is linked directly.

Foundational Books

These are the core texts in the field — between them they cover history, chemistry, plant sources, mordanting, and technique in depth. If you read only a few books, start here.

Jenny Dean — Wild Colour: How to Make and Use Natural Dyes ISBN: 978-1-78472-553-2 (Pavilion Books, 2018 revised ed.) The most accessible all-round introduction: plant profiles, mordant guides, and step-by-step dyebath instructions. Referenced throughout Step 07 — Plant Dye Sources, Step 06 — Mordants — Making Colour Stick, and Step 10 — The Dyebath — A Step-by-Step Guide. Amazon listing

India Flint — Eco Colour: Botanical Dyes for Beautiful Textiles ISBN: 978-1-59668-330-3 (Interweave Press, 2008) The book that introduced eco printing as a named practice to a wide audience. Essential for Step 03 — What Is Eco Printing, Step 13 — Choosing Plants for Eco Printing, Step 14 — Bundling and Steaming Techniques, and Step 15 — Eco Printing on Paper. Amazon listing

Dominique Cardon — Natural Dyes: Sources, Tradition, Technology and Science ISBN: 978-1-904982-00-5 (Archetype Publications, 2007) The encyclopaedic scholarly reference — 778 pages covering biological descriptions, historical recipes, and full chemistry of every major natural dye. The definitive source for Step 01 — Natural Dye in History and Culture, Step 04 — The Science of Colour and Fibre, Step 08 — Insects and Animal-Derived Dyes, and Step 09 — Iron, Tannins, and Mineral Dyes. Publisher listing via Maiwa

Catharine Ellis & Joy Boutrup — The Art and Science of Natural Dyes: Principles, Experiments, and Results ISBN: 978-0-7643-5633-9 (Schiffer Publishing, 2019) A rigorous, experiment-driven guide aimed at dyers who want to understand why things work. Key reference for Step 04 — The Science of Colour and Fibre, Step 11 — Colour Modifiers and Afterbaths, Step 12 — Achieving Colour Range and Depth, and Step 16 — Fermentation Vats — Indigo and Beyond. AbeBooks listing

History and Culture

Relevant to Step 01 — Natural Dye in History and Culture.

  • “Natural Dyes: The History of Extraction of Beautiful Plant Pigments” — Marasim Blog. Covers madder in the Indus civilisation (c. 3000 BC), Tutankhamun’s grave goods, and ancient Chinese use. marasim.co

  • “Natural Colorants: Historical, Processing and Sustainable Prospects” — PMC / Natural Products and Bioprospecting (2017). Open-access review covering henna, kermes, indigo, and madder in ancient Egypt and beyond. PMC link

  • “Natural Dyes in Historical Egyptian Textiles” — Lupine Publishers: Fashion Technology & Textile Engineering. Describes madder-dyed textile fragments from Tutankhamun’s tomb. lupinepublishers.com

  • “Meet Madder, Woad and Weld: Traditional Dye Plants of Europe” — Low Impact Living. Practical overview of the three great European dye plants and their archaeological record. lowimpact.org

  • “Medieval Dye Plants Discovered in Belgium for the First Time” — Medievalists.net (2025). Reports archaeological traces of weld, madder, and woad in medieval Belgian cloth production. medievalists.net

Science of Colour and Mordanting

Relevant to Step 04 — The Science of Colour and Fibre and Step 06 — Mordants — Making Colour Stick.

  • Maiwa Guide to Natural Dyes — Comprehensive free online guide from Maiwa Handprints (Vancouver) covering water, fibres, mordants in three parts, and individual dye plants. An excellent companion to the foundational books. maiwahandprints.blogspot.com

  • Ellis, C. & Boutrup, J. — The Art and Science of Natural Dyes (see Foundational Books above) — Chapters on mordant chemistry are especially relevant here.

Plant Dye Sources

Relevant to Step 07 — Plant Dye Sources.

  • “Plant-Based Dyes: Indigo, Madder, and Weld” — Mother Earth Gardener. Profiles the three most historically important plant dyes with growing notes. motherearthgardener.com

  • Jenny Dean’s Wild Colour website — Contact Printing on Fabric and Paper jennydean.co.uk

Insects and Animal-Derived Dyes

Relevant to Step 08 — Insects and Animal-Derived Dyes.

  • “Cochineal” — Encyclopædia Britannica. Overview of cochineal biology, history of Spanish introduction to Europe, and modern uses. britannica.com

  • Akçay, A. A. — “The Use of Insect Pigment in Art Works” — Insects 15(7):519 (2024). DOI: 10.3390/insects15070519. Open-access scholarly article covering carmine, cochineal, lac, and kermes across art history. PMC link

Eco Printing

Fermentation Vats and Indigo

Relevant to Step 16 — Fermentation Vats — Indigo and Beyond.

  • Miyazaki, Y. & Murase, A. — “Fermentation Blues: Analyzing the Microbiota of Traditional Indigo Vat Dyeing in Hunan, China” — Microbiology Spectrum 10(4) (2022). DOI: 10.1128/spectrum.01663-22. Open-access article examining the bacterial communities that drive indigo reduction in traditional Chinese vats. ASM Journals

  • “The Fermented Indigo Vat: An Introduction” — Ellis Textiles blog (Joy Boutrup & Catharine Ellis). Companion to their book; discusses the practical science of fermentation vats. blog.ellistextiles.com

  • “The Revival of Natural Indigo Dyeing: Techniques and Global Impact” — Textile School. Overview of the global indigo revival and the reduction chemistry. textileschool.com

Resist Dyeing

Relevant to Step 17 — Resist Dyeing Techniques.

  • “Shibori: Japan’s Language of Cloth” — Wandering Silk. History of Japanese shibori and its relationship to resist traditions in Central Asia, Peru, and China. wanderingsilk.org

  • “Batik vs. Shibori: Understanding Resist-Dyeing Techniques in Sustainable Fashion” — Anuprerna. Compares the two traditions and traces Indian parallel heritage (Dabu, Gujarat wax resist). anuprerna.com

Lightfastness

Relevant to Step 18 — Lightfastness and Caring for Naturally Dyed Items.

  • “A Treatment to Reduce Fading and Degradation of Textiles” — University of Nebraska Digital Commons (peer-reviewed). Compares fading of indigo, turmeric, and other natural dyes under UV exposure; shows turmeric fades most (ΔE = 59) and indigo least (ΔE = 1.3). digitalcommons.unl.edu

  • “Characteristics of Fading of Wool Cloth Dyed with Selected Natural Dyestuffs on the Basis of Solar Radiant Energy” — Dyes and Pigments 59(3) (2003). DOI: 10.1016/S0143-7208(03)00065-2. Scholarly study showing most natural dyes have poorer lightfastness than AATCC Blue Wool L2 standard. ScienceDirect

  • “Improving Light Fastness of Natural Dyes on Cotton Yarn” — Scribd / technical paper. Demonstrates that vitamin C and gallic acid as additives improve lightfastness across multiple natural dyes. scribd.com

Sustainability and Ethics

Relevant to Step 19 — Sustainability and Ethics in Natural Dyeing.

  • “Extraction of Eco-Friendly Natural Dyes and Biomordants for Textile Coloration: A Critical Review” — Sustainable Chemistry and Pharmacy (2024). DOI: 10.1016/j.scp.2024.101549. Reviews environmental advantages of natural dyes and biomordants over synthetics. ScienceDirect

  • “Natural Versus Synthetic Dyes: Consumers’ Understanding of Apparel Coloration and Their Willingness to Adopt Sustainable Alternatives” — Cleaner and Responsible Consumption (2023). DOI: 10.1016/j.clrc.2023.100088. Explores consumer attitudes and the complexity of the ‘natural = sustainable’ assumption. ScienceDirect

Gyotaku — Fish Printing

Relevant to Step 21 — Gyotaku — Fish Printing.

  • “Traditional Japanese Fish Art Could Be a Boon for Conservation” — Smithsonian Magazine. Reports on researchers Miyazaki and Murase collecting 261 gyotaku, including prints dating to 1839 (Edo period). smithsonianmag.com

  • “Gyotaku — Japan’s Fish Printing Culture from the Edo Period to the Present” — WTF Wasabi. Detailed history including the oldest surviving gyotaku (“The Crucian Carp of Kinshi-bori”, February 1839, Lord Sakai Tadayoshi). wtfwasabi.com

  • “Gyotaku Fish Printing” — Kennedy Center Education Resources. K–2 lesson plan providing a concise historical overview: “Gyotaku (gyo = fish, taku = rubbing) was invented in the early 1800s in Japan by fishermen who wanted to record their catch.” kennedy-center.org

  • “How the Traditional Japanese Art of Fish Printing Inspired a Modern Art Form” — Atlas Obscura. Profiles the form’s history including the contested earliest example (a carp from the Mogami River, 1857, held at the Honma Museum, Yamagata). atlasobscura.com
