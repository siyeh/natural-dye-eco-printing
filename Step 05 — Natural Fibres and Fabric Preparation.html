Step 05 — Natural Fibres and Fabric Preparation

Before a single drop of dye touches your fabric, what that fabric is made of — and how you’ve prepared it — will determine almost everything about the final result. This note walks you through choosing the right fibre and getting it ready to receive colour.

Choosing Your Fibre

Natural dyes work on natural fibres. Synthetic fabrics (polyester, nylon, acrylic) simply don’t absorb plant-based pigments in any meaningful way, so always start with 100% natural material.

Wool is the beginner’s best friend. Its scaly protein structure grabs mordants and dyes eagerly, producing deep, saturated colours with relatively little effort. It’s also forgiving — minor temperature fluctuations won’t ruin it (though agitation will cause it to felt, so handle gently).

Silk takes dye even more intensely than wool and produces luminous, jewel-like colours. It’s more delicate and more expensive, but if you want to see what a dye is truly capable of, silk will show you.

Cotton and linen are trickier. Their cellulose structure has fewer natural bonding sites for most dyes (see Step 04 — The Science of Colour and Fibre for why). You can still get beautiful results, but you’ll need to do more preparation work — specifically, a tannin pre-treatment or a higher mordant load.

Alpaca, cashmere, and other protein fibres behave similarly to wool and are excellent choices once you’re past the beginner stage.

Rayon — The Semi-Synthetic Cellulose Fibre

Rayon occupies an unusual middle ground in the fibre world. It is a manufactured fibre made from regenerated cellulose — plant material (typically wood pulp from beech, pine, eucalyptus, or bamboo) that has been dissolved into a liquid and re-spun into new fibres. Because it starts as a plant but is heavily transformed by chemistry, rayon is considered semi-synthetic: not a natural fibre, but not a fully synthetic one like polyester either.

The most common form is viscose rayon, known for its soft, drapey, silky hand. Two upgraded versions are worth knowing: Modal, a denser and more dimensionally stable viscose, and Lyocell, most widely sold under the brand name Tencel — which gets its own section below.

How Rayon Fits the Fibre Matrix

Despite its industrial origins, rayon’s regenerated cellulose structure behaves chemically almost identically to cotton in the dyebath. That means it sits firmly in the cellulose family alongside cotton and linen, and requires the same preparation approach:

  • Scouring — essential, as with all fibres.
  • Tannin pre-treatment — recommended before mordanting, just as with cotton, to give the fibre extra bonding sites.
  • Mordanting — required. Alum (potassium aluminium sulphate) at 15–20% WOF works well. Rayon’s high absorbency means it can take up mordants and dyes readily, often producing rich, vibrant colour.

One important difference from cotton: rayon fibres are noticeably weaker when wet and can lose their shape if handled roughly. Keep your dyebath at a gentle simmer rather than a rolling boil, avoid prolonged agitation, and rinse carefully.

A Note on Sustainability

Rayon’s environmental story is complicated. The raw material is renewable plant cellulose, but the production process is energy- and water-intensive, and some supply chains have been linked to deforestation. If sustainability is a priority for you (explored in depth in Step 19 — Sustainability and Ethics in Natural Dyeing), seek out FSC-certified viscose or favour Lyocell/Tencel, whose closed-loop process is far cleaner.

Tencel — Lyocell’s Better Half

Tencel is the brand name owned by Austrian company Lenzing AG for their lyocell fibre, and it has become so widely used that many people treat “Tencel” and “lyocell” as synonyms. Like rayon, it is made from wood-pulp cellulose — typically from sustainably managed eucalyptus, beech, or oak. The crucial difference is how that cellulose is turned into fibre.

Viscose rayon uses harsh chemicals (notably carbon disulfide) in an open-loop process where a significant proportion of the solvent is lost as industrial waste. Tencel/lyocell uses a non-toxic organic solvent called NMMO (N-methylmorpholine N-oxide) in a closed-loop process: approximately 99% of the solvent is recovered, purified, and fed back into the system. Almost no chemical waste escapes. This makes Tencel the most environmentally responsible choice among the regenerated cellulose fibres — and arguably among all fibres commonly available to dyers.

What it Feels and Acts Like

Tencel has a silky softness that rivals viscose rayon, but it is considerably stronger — including when wet. Where a viscose scarf can feel fragile and distort easily in the dyebath, a Tencel scarf retains its shape and handles moderate heat without complaint. It is also breathable and highly moisture-absorbent, both of which make it an excellent candidate for natural dyeing.

Dyeing Tencel

In the dyebath, Tencel behaves like all other cellulose fibres. The preparation sequence is the same as for cotton:

  1. Scour in warm water with a pH-neutral soap.
  2. Tannin pre-treat (oak gall, black tea, or sumac) to build bonding sites.
  3. Mordant with alum at 15–20% WOF, simmering gently for 45–60 minutes.
  4. Dye at a moderate simmer — no need to baby it the way you would viscose.

Tencel takes colour evenly and cleanly, often producing particularly clear, bright results. Its superior wet strength means you can stir and move it in the dyebath without anxiety, and you’ll find it resists patchiness better than cotton when conditions aren’t perfect.

Scouring — The Essential First Step

Scouring means washing your fabric thoroughly before dyeing. Even “new” fabric from a shop carries sizing (a stiffening agent applied during manufacture), oils from spinning, and handling residues — all of which form a barrier between the fibre and the dye. If you skip scouring, colour uptake will be patchy and uneven.

For protein fibres, simmer gently in warm water with a small amount of pH-neutral dish soap or dedicated wool wash for 30–45 minutes. Avoid sudden temperature changes and agitation. Rinse thoroughly.

For cellulose fibres (including rayon and Tencel), you can use a slightly higher temperature and a little soda ash (sodium carbonate) to remove plant waxes and oils more effectively. Handle viscose rayon especially gently — avoid wringing or bunching while wet. Tencel can tolerate a little more movement.

After scouring, your fabric should feel clean and slightly limp, with no slippery or stiff patches.

Tannin Pre-Treatment for Cotton, Linen, Rayon, and Tencel

Tannins are plant compounds that bond naturally to cellulose and also attract metallic mordants. By soaking cotton, linen, rayon, or Tencel in a tannin solution before mordanting, you’re essentially giving the fibre the bonding sites it was born without.

Common tannin sources include oak galls, black tea, sumac leaves, and pomegranate rind. Simmer the tannin material in water, strain, then add your wet, scoured fabric and simmer for an hour. Rinse and proceed to mordanting. You’ll find tannins discussed further in Step 09 — Iron, Tannins, and Mineral Dyes.

Mordanting

After scouring (and tannin treatment if needed), the fabric is ready to mordant. Most dyers mordant before dyeing — this is called pre-mordanting — though mordanting during or after the dyebath is also possible and produces subtly different results.

The standard alum mordant bath: dissolve potassium aluminium sulphate in hot water at roughly 15–20% weight of fibre (WOF), add your wet fabric, and simmer for 45–60 minutes. Remove, squeeze gently, and the fabric is ready for the dyebath. Full mordant options and ratios are in Step 06 — Mordants — Making Colour Stick.

Keeping Notes

From your very first dye session, record what fibre you used, how you scoured it, what mordant and percentage you applied, and what dye you used. Colour is reproducible only if you know what you did. A simple notebook or a note in this thoughtbase works perfectly.
